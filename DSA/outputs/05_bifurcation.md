# 05 Convergence versus divergence

The answer stated as conditions, not a single number.

## The crossover surface
Two assumptions the ratio turns on: post-2028 sustained growth, and the composition of the
consolidation (the share falling on capital spending, which lowers potential growth through a
public-investment channel). The frontier that separates a declining debt path (2033 below 2029) from
a rising one, read off `data/analysis.json`:

| Share of the cut on capex | Minimum sustained growth for a declining path |
|---|---|
| 0.0 (all on current spending) | about 0.75 percent |
| 0.3 | about 1.0 percent |
| 0.6 (the program's stated tilt) | about 1.25 percent |
| 1.0 (all on capex) | about 1.75 percent |

The composition moves the required growth by about one percentage point across its range. Cutting
investment rather than current spending raises the growth the economy must sustain merely to keep
deleveraging, from about 0.75 to about 1.75 percent. The S0 baseline sits at about 2.75 percent
post-2028 growth with roughly 0.6 of the cut on capex, inside the declining region with a cushion of
about 1.5 percentage points of growth on this criterion.

## Minimum growth for the program's own target
The stricter criterion is the program's own: keep the ratio below 90 percent by 2029. Given the
programmed primary path, the minimum sustained post-2028 growth that holds the market-rate ratio below
90 percent in 2029 is 1.7 percent. The program assumes about 2.5 to 3 percent. The cushion on this
criterion is about one percentage point. Below 1.7 percent growth the market-rate ratio stays above 90
in 2029 (breaching the stated path) while still declining slowly, because the carry stays negative.
The growth sensitivity: at 0 percent post-2028 growth the 2029 ratio is about 84 / 93 (official /
market); at 1 percent, 82 / 91; at 2 percent, 81 / 89; at 2.75 percent, 80 / 88. The market-rate 90
line is crossed between 1 and 2 percent growth, consistent with the 1.7-percent threshold.

## Does the baseline require a primary surplus?
No, on the baseline. The stabilizing primary balance pb\* is negative from 2027 onward (minus 4.8 in
2027 to minus 1.4 by 2035, `exhibits/tab_rminusg.md`), so the programmed zero primary from 2029 is
more than enough for the ratio to fall once the 2026 revaluation is in the stock. A primary surplus
becomes necessary only if the negative carry is removed. Two channels remove it:
- **Disinflation plus repricing.** The program targets single-digit inflation by 2028 (MEFP p.10) and
  issues external and local debt at market rates; if r minus g turns to about plus 1 percent, pb\*
  turns to about a plus 0.9 percent of GDP surplus on an 85-percent stock. This is the program's own
  success removing its own tailwind.
- **Stock shocks.** A contingent-liability crystallization of 10 percent of GDP puts the 2029 ratio at
  about 90.1 percent official (above 90 at market); a second depreciation of 25 percent with an FX
  share of 0.40 puts it at about 89 percent official (above 90 at market). Either pushes the ratio
  back to or above the 90 line even with the negative flow carry intact.

## Statement of the bifurcation
On the program's assumptions the path converges. It diverges, or stalls above 90, under a specific and
identifiable combination: the disinflation and rate normalization the program intends, removing the
negative carry, occurring together with a growth shortfall below about 1.7 percent, and with the
capital-heavy consolidation the program's delay-and-cancel language points to. Each element alone is
survivable; the combination is where the ratio stops falling and a primary surplus of the order of 1
percent of GDP becomes the condition for renewed decline. The program's baseline is on the convergent
side of the frontier, with a growth cushion of about one percentage point and no cushion at all
against a large contingent-liability crystallization.
